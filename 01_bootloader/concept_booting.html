What Even Is Booting?

You say "just reboot it" a dozen times a week. But "booting" names one of the strangest problems in all of computing: how does a machine with a completely empty head — no OS, no program, nothing in RAM — ever get its first instruction running?

01 — The Big Analogy

Head First Moment

Imagine waking up with total amnesia, in a pitch-black room, with no memory of who you are, where you are, or what to do next — but taped to your own hand is a single sticky note with one instruction on it: "Stand up, then read the note taped to the wall." That note gets you to a slightly bigger note, which gets you to a full instruction manual, which finally gets you to "here's your name, your house, your life — go live it."

That's booting. The CPU wakes up with total amnesia every single time power is applied. It doesn't remember yesterday. It doesn't know an operating system exists. All it has is one hardwired reflex: "go read the byte at this exact address, and do whatever it says." Everything else — your desktop, your browser, this very document — is the end of a long chain of notes, each one leading to the next.

Booting is the umbrella word for that entire chain, start to finish: from the instant electricity reaches the CPU to the moment you see a login screen, a shell prompt, or a desktop. It is not one program. It is not one component. It is a process — a relay race with several different runners, each handing a baton to the next.

02 — Cold Boot vs. Warm Boot vs. Sleep/Resume

Not every "starting up" is the same kind of starting up. This trips people up constantly, so let's separate the three you actually encounter:

Cold Boot

Power was fully off. RAM has lost all state (RAM is volatile — no power, no data). The entire chain in §03 below runs from scratch: BIOS/UEFI does full POST, full device re-detection, everything.

Warm Boot (Reboot)

Power technically never dropped — the OS asked the firmware to restart execution. Some hardware self-tests are shortened, but the software chain (firmware → bootloader → kernel) still runs in full. This is what Ctrl+Alt+Del historically triggered.

Sleep / Resume

Not a boot at all. RAM stays powered (S3 sleep) or its contents are dumped to disk first (hibernate/S4). On resume, the CPU jumps back into a running OS that was frozen mid-thought — none of the chain in §03 re-executes.

Why this distinction matters for BobOS

Everything you're building in this repo — 01_bootloader onward — only exists because of cold boot. QEMU launching fresh each time you type make run is simulating the full amnesia-to-awareness chain, every single time, on purpose. That repetition is the entire point: it's the one part of the whole OS you get to watch happen from absolute zero.

04 — Where the Word Came From (Short Version)

boot is short for bootstrap — and that word carries a genuinely interesting engineering idea buried inside an old, physically-impossible idiom. It deserves its own full treatment rather than a rushed paragraph here — that's exactly what concept_bootstrapping.html is for. The short version: computer engineers in the 1950s borrowed the phrase "pull yourself up by your own bootstraps" (meaning: to succeed using only your own resources, with no outside help) to describe a machine starting itself with no operator-loaded program — because that's exactly how impossible it originally sounded.

05 — Brain Exercise

Sharpen Your Pencil

Before reading on: if RAM loses everything the instant power drops, and the CPU has no built-in operating system, how does it know to look at address 0xFFFFFFF0 specifically, rather than any other address? Is that a software decision anyone could have made differently, or something else entirely?

Hint: nothing has executed yet at that point — so the answer can't be "a program decided this." Think about what's true of a circuit before any instruction has ever run.

There Are No Dumb Questions

Q: Is "boot" just short for "bootstrap," or is there more to it?
Both are true at once. It's literally an abbreviation — but the abbreviation stuck specifically because "bootstrapping" describes the actual mechanism (tiny thing loads bigger thing, over and over), not just a cute nickname. See concept_bootstrapping.html.
Q: Is the BIOS "the boot"?
No — the BIOS is one participant in booting, specifically the firmware that runs first. "Booting" is the whole trip; BIOS is the first leg of it. Full breakdown in concept_bios.html.
Q: Why does a cold boot take longer than resuming from sleep?
Because sleep skips the entire chain in §03 — RAM already holds a fully-initialized OS. A cold boot has to rebuild that state from nothing, running every stage in order.

06 — How This Connects to the Other Three Concepts

The Short Version

Booting is the whole event. Bootstrapping is the underlying principle that explains why booting needs multiple stages at all. BIOS is the firmware that runs the first stage. Bootloader is the program — the one you write — that BIOS loads and hands control to next.
