The last lesson showed that a free list works. This one asks the harder question: when three, four, five free chunks are all big enough for a request, which one do you actually hand out? The answer isn't obvious, and every choice quietly bills you later — in speed, in wasted bytes, or in a heap riddled with slivers nothing can use.

00 — Setting the Scene

The Question kmalloc() Never Gets to Skip

Picture the free list mid-program, after a realistic mix of allocations and frees. It isn't one tidy chunk anymore — it's a scattered handful of leftover pieces of different sizes:

Position in free listChunkSize (bytes)
1stP20
2ndQ200
3rdR48
4thS60
5thT500

Now a request comes in: kmalloc(50). Chunks Q, S, and T are all large enough (P and R are too small, at 20 and 48 bytes). Three candidates qualify. Which one does the allocator pick? This single decision — made fresh on every single allocation, thousands of times over a kernel's lifetime — is what separates the three classic strategies this lesson covers. Same free list, same request, three different outcomes.

Why This Isn't a Minor Detail
It's tempting to think "any correct chunk works, so who cares which one gets picked." But the choice compounds. Pick badly a thousand times in a row and you don't get a thousand independent small mistakes — you get a heap shaped by a thousand compounding decisions, which is exactly how allocators end up either fast-but-wasteful or careful-but-slow. There is no free lunch here, only different bills.
01 — Strategy One

First-Fit: Take the First One That Works

First-fit is the strategy you'd invent if nobody told you there was a choice to make: walk the free list from the head, and the instant you find a chunk big enough, stop and use it. No further looking. Applied to the table above, requesting 50 bytes, first-fit scans P (20, too small), Q (200, big enough — stop here). It never even looks at R, S, or T.

C — first-fit searchstruct block_header *find_first_fit(uint32_t size) {
    struct block_header *blk = heap_start;
    while (blk) {
        if (blk->free && blk->size >= size) {
            return blk;   // stop at the FIRST fit — don't keep looking
        }
        blk = blk->next;
    }
    return NULL;  // out of memory
}

Split into 50 used + 150 free remainder, chunk Q becomes Q₁ (50, used) followed immediately by Q₂ (150, free), and the free list now reads P, Q₂, R, S, T. Notice what just happened to Q's leftover: it's still perfectly usable — 150 bytes is plenty for future requests — so this particular split isn't the problem case. The trouble with first-fit shows up over many allocations of small sizes clustered near the head of the list.

The Real Cost of First-Fit
Because first-fit always starts scanning from the head, and small leftover slivers tend to accumulate near the front of the list (repeatedly split, repeatedly slightly-too-small for the next request), later searches waste time walking past a graveyard of tiny unusable fragments before reaching a chunk that's actually large enough. The search is cheap in the best case — sometimes literally the very first chunk — but the quality of what accumulates near the head degrades over the program's lifetime.

02 — Strategy Two

Best-Fit: Search Everything, Waste the Least

Best-fit refuses to settle for the first candidate — it walks the entire free list, considers every chunk that's big enough, and picks whichever one leaves the smallest leftover remainder. Applied to the same 50-byte request over P/Q/R/S/T: Q (200), S (60), and T (500) all qualify. Their remainders after taking 50 bytes would be 150, 10, and 450 respectively. Best-fit picks S — the 60-byte chunk — because a 10-byte remainder is the smallest waste of the three.

C — best-fit searchstruct block_header *find_best_fit(uint32_t size) {
    struct block_header *blk = heap_start;
    struct block_header *best = NULL;
    while (blk) {
        if (blk->free && blk->size >= size) {
            if (!best || blk->size < best->size) {
                best = blk;   // tightest fit seen so far
            }
        }
        blk = blk->next;   // MUST check every chunk — no early exit
    }
    return best;
}

On paper this sounds strictly better — why would you ever want to waste more space than you have to? The catch is twofold. First, the search itself is more expensive: first-fit can stop the moment it finds any candidate, but best-fit has no early exit — it must walk the entire list every single time, even when the very first chunk it saw turns out to be optimal. Second, and more subtly damaging: best-fit's whole strategy is to leave the smallest possible leftover — which means it systematically manufactures tiny remainders. A 10-byte sliver like the one left behind in S is too small to satisfy almost any future request, and now it's a permanent, useless resident of the free list.

The Irony of "Optimal" Per-Allocation Choices
Best-fit optimizes each individual decision — minimize this one allocation's waste — but that greedy local optimum is exactly what produces the worst long-term litter of unusable fragments. Minimizing waste per chunk is not the same goal as minimizing the total number of useless fragments in the heap over time, and best-fit chases the former while quietly making the latter worse.
Worth knowing: despite the fragmentation downside, best-fit is genuinely used in production — it's one of the strategies available in dlmalloc-family allocators for certain size classes, particularly when memory is tight and search cost matters less than squeezing out every usable byte. There's no universally "correct" strategy; it's a trade-off selected per workload.
03 — Strategy Three

Worst-Fit: Take the Biggest, On Purpose

Worst-fit sounds like a joke until you see the reasoning: deliberately choose the largest free chunk available, on the theory that a big leftover remainder is far more likely to be useful for some future request than a small one. Over the same table, worst-fit compares Q (200), S (60), and T (500), and picks T — leaving a 450-byte remainder behind, which is roomy enough to satisfy plenty of future allocations without needing to be split again.

C — worst-fit searchstruct block_header *find_worst_fit(uint32_t size) {
    struct block_header *blk = heap_start;
    struct block_header *worst = NULL;
    while (blk) {
        if (blk->free && blk->size >= size) {
            if (!worst || blk->size > worst->size) {
                worst = blk;   // LARGEST fit seen so far
            }
        }
        blk = blk->next;   // also a full scan, like best-fit
    }
    return worst;
}

Worst-fit shares best-fit's cost profile — a full O(n) scan every time, no early exit — but flips the outcome philosophy entirely. Instead of minimizing this allocation's leftover, it maximizes it, betting that a big remainder stays useful longer than a small one would. In workloads where allocation sizes vary a lot and unpredictably, this can genuinely reduce how often the allocator gets stuck with a pile of same-sized unusable slivers, because it's actively avoiding the creation of small remainders in the first place.

Why Worst-Fit Is the Least Common of the Three
The bet doesn't always pay off. If allocation sizes are fairly uniform, worst-fit ends up repeatedly carving down the same large chunk (or whatever's currently largest) — and once the largest chunks are all whittled down, you're right back to a free list of mid-sized leftovers, just having spent more CPU cycles per allocation to get there than first-fit would have. It's a real, historically documented strategy, but production general-purpose allocators rarely choose it as their default.

05 — The Bill Comes Due

Two Kinds of Fragmentation

"Fragmentation" gets used loosely, but it names two genuinely different problems, and every strategy above trades one against the other.

External Fragmentation

Enough free memory exists in total to satisfy a request, but it's scattered across chunks too small individually. Picture a free list of five 10-byte chunks (50 bytes free, total) and a request for 40 bytes — it fails, even though 50 > 40, because no single chunk is big enough. The memory isn't gone; it's just unusably scattered.

Internal Fragmentation

A chunk handed out is bigger than what was actually requested, and the difference is wasted inside the allocation itself — invisible to the free list, because that space isn't free, it's just unused. This happens whenever an allocator rounds sizes up (for alignment, or because splitting further wasn't worth it) or deliberately avoids splitting a chunk that's only slightly larger than requested.
Where each strategy lands Best-fit minimizes internal fragmentation per allocation, but maximizes external fragmentation over time (tiny unusable slivers pile up). Worst-fit does roughly the opposite. First-fit sits in between, biased toward whichever end of the list gets scanned first. No strategy eliminates both kinds at once — that's the actual, unavoidable trade-off this whole lesson has been building toward.

A Worked External-Fragmentation Example

Concrete numbers make this stick better than the general statement does. Suppose a run of best-fit allocations has left the free list looking like this:

Free chunkSizeCan it satisfy kmalloc(32)?
W9 BNo — too small
X11 BNo — too small
Y7 BNo — too small
Z5 BNo — too small

Total free memory: 9 + 11 + 7 + 5 = 32 bytes exactly — precisely enough, in aggregate, to satisfy a 32-byte request. And yet kmalloc(32) fails, because no single chunk reaches 32 bytes on its own, and none of these four chunks are physically adjacent (so forward coalescing, which only merges neighbors, can't help either). This is external fragmentation in its purest, most unambiguous form: the memory hasn't leaked, it hasn't vanished — it's simply scattered too finely to be usable.

The Diagnostic Question
Whenever an allocation fails, ask: is heap_get_free_bytes() (bob_os's diagnostic from heap.h) still comfortably larger than the request? If yes, this is external fragmentation, not genuine exhaustion — the fix is better coalescing or a smarter placement strategy, not more physical memory. If heap_get_free_bytes() is itself smaller than the request, that's real out-of-memory, and no allocation strategy in this lesson can rescue it.

Go back to bob_os's real heap.c from the first lesson in this module: recall the MIN_SPLIT constant, which refuses to split a chunk if the leftover would be smaller than 16 bytes, instead handing the caller the whole chunk. That's a deliberate, explicit acceptance of internal fragmentation — "a few wasted bytes inside this allocation is a better trade than an unusable 3-byte sliver haunting the free list forever."

    // ...split into used + free remainder...

06 — The Standard Mitigation

Coalescing: Undoing Fragmentation After the Fact

Every strategy above eventually produces some external fragmentation — it's a question of how much and how fast, not whether. The standard fix isn't a smarter search; it's cleanup after the fact: when a chunk is freed, check whether the chunk physically next to it (or, in a fancier allocator, physically before it too) is also free, and if so, merge them into one larger chunk on the spot.

C — forward coalescing, from bob_os/08_heap/heap.cvoid kfree(void *ptr) {
    struct block_header *blk = (struct block_header *)ptr - 1;
    blk->flags = BLOCK_FREE;

    // If the NEXT block is also free, merge them into one.
    // Repeat in case several consecutive free blocks now exist.
    struct block_header *next = (struct block_header *)((char*)blk + HDR_SIZE + blk->size);
    while (next->flags == BLOCK_FREE && next->size > 0) {
        blk->size += HDR_SIZE + next->size;
        next = (struct block_header *)((char*)blk + HDR_SIZE + blk->size);
    }
}

Coalescing directly attacks external fragmentation: instead of five scattered 10-byte free chunks sitting inert forever, adjacent ones get glued back together the moment both halves become free, regenerating larger usable chunks out of the wreckage that splitting left behind. It's the single most important reason a long-running allocator doesn't slowly grind to a halt, unable to satisfy requests it should easily be able to fill.

Why Only "Forward" Coalescing Isn't the Full Story
bob_os's kfree() only checks the next physical block — it never checks whether the block before the one just freed is also free. That's a real, acknowledged limitation (bob_os's own tutorial calls it out as a "Sharpen Your Pencil" exercise): production allocators like glibc's ptmalloc coalesce in both directions, using a technique called boundary tags — a second, trailing copy of the header at the very end of each chunk, so a block can cheaply check its previous neighbor without needing a full backward scan. Module 08's simpler forward-only coalescing is intentionally a subset of the real technique, not a different one.

Put together, this is the full lifecycle every kernel-heap byte goes through: a chunk gets split (creating some internal fragmentation, and possibly a small new free chunk), gets handed out, eventually gets freed, and — if its neighbor is also free — gets coalesced back into something larger. Strategy decides which chunk gets split each time; coalescing decides how much of that splitting's damage gets undone afterward. Neither one alone is the whole picture.

07 — Quick Reference

Glossary & Key Terms

First-Fit
Use the first free chunk encountered that's large enough. Cheap search, but slivers tend to accumulate near the head of the list.
Best-Fit
Scan the whole list, pick the smallest chunk that still fits. Minimizes waste per allocation, but manufactures many tiny unusable fragments over time.
Worst-Fit
Scan the whole list, deliberately pick the largest chunk. Leaves big, probably-useful remainders, at the cost of a full scan every time.
External Fragmentation
Enough total free memory exists, but it's scattered across chunks too small individually to satisfy a request.
Internal Fragmentation
A handed-out chunk is larger than what was requested; the unused difference is wasted but invisible to the free list.
Coalescing
Merging two adjacent free chunks back into one larger chunk, typically performed at free-time, to undo external fragmentation.
Boundary Tags
A trailing copy of a chunk's header placed at its end, letting an allocator check its previous physical neighbor without a backward scan — enables bidirectional coalescing.
Split
Dividing a free chunk larger than requested into a used front piece and a smaller free remainder, when that remainder is large enough to be worth keeping.
